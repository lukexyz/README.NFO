  <b>CASTAWAY</b> <i>(working title)</i>: ten hours on one tiny island, with one tall palm, one raft and one young woman in headphones, nodding to the beat.<br>
  Keep one hand on the wall and you always get out, eventually. This is a video about eventually.

Castaway is a stationary-frame lo-fi video for YouTube, in the spirit of the ten-hour lofi streams, and an unofficial remake inspired by *Johnny Castaway*, the 1992 desert-island screensaver: its small-island routines and visual comedy, repainted as a sunny, hand-painted coastal scene. 16:9, 1080p, 30 fps, and always daytime. She mostly idles. Every so often, something happens: a message in a bottle washes straight back; a drone delivers a parcel, and the parcel is another pair of headphones; a coconut falls on a hermit crab, and the crab walks off wearing it. [activities.toml](activities.toml) puts more than 90 activities on four timers, and every one of them waits for the next bar of the music, every 3 seconds, so the gags land on the beat. Every sound is synthesized from code by [tools/make_audio.py](tools/make_audio.py): no samples, no loops, no recordings.

```sh
python tools/serve.py
# then open http://127.0.0.1:8765/
```

Live preview in the page, then export a YouTube-ready MP4: the browser encodes frame-exact H.264 (WebCodecs, 68 to 78 frames a second at 1080p30 in Chrome), and the server mixes in the sound and joins the two. Plain ES modules, no build step, no npm packages. In development: no video has been published yet.

The banner walks exactly this route. The maze is carved by a depth-first walk from a generator seeded with 1992, the video's default seed, and it happens to have a corridor eight cells long along the bottom: one cell for every letter. The crab does not follow any walls. It is at the exit in seven and a half seconds and nobody knows how.

Counts are medians of 200 simulated 10-hour runs, as [activities.toml](activities.toml) says at the top; `python tools/schedule.py` checks the file and simulates a run of its own. Lanes let things overlap, which is how a ship gets to sail past exactly while she is busy with a coconut (the ship even waits for her to get busy). Also on the list: a tour boat of selfie-takers, a bro on an electric hydrofoil who throws a shaka and carves off, a cat who climbs the palm, naps, and one day floats away on the crate again (and comes back another time), bushcraft (fire by friction, a hammock, a lookout up the palm, spear fishing), and a kumara, planted in the sand, that grows over the course of the video.

Scene life runs underneath on its own: 26 entries, 4 always on and 22 timed. The shore waves and the drifting cloud shadows are built; distant birds, planes with vapour trails, whale pods, dolphins, sailboats, sandpipers, a gecko and a rain shower are planned. `python tools/render_demo.py --dev` renders a reel of every activity with a heads-up display.

<summary><b>HOW THE BANNER WALKS</b>: a raycaster, 82 frames and one roll</summary>
<br>

The maze screensavers of the mid-1990s drew their corridors with real perspective, which an SVG cannot do: its transforms are flat. So the banner's generator, a dependency-free Node script, carries a tiny raycaster of its own. It renders the walk at 160 by 100 pixels with nearest-neighbour textures it draws itself: brick walls, floorboards, speckled ceiling tiles, four framed pictures and two life rings. The crab and the sun are flat sprites, the rock is a little flat-shaded polyhedron, and everything is depth-tested against the walls, so the crab really does disappear round corners. The sun is see-through by palette: each of its pixels is the colour behind it, mixed with sun.

One frame every sixth of a second, three frames to a step or a turn: stepped movement, which happens to be the video's own motion default. The 82 frames that differ go into one tall PNG, a film strip, and a CSS keyframe list slides it along one frame at a time. The strip is stored out of order, each frame next to the one it most resembles, because a corridor one cell further on looks almost exactly like the corridor before, and the compressor only remembers about one frame back. That alone takes the strip from about 140 KB to under 90.

The rock does not turn the frames over. The frames are always rendered the right way up, and touching the rock rolls the whole view 180 degrees, zoomed in mid-roll so the corners never show. That is also why the walker turns left into the dead end: the screensaver swapped to the left hand after a rock, and so does this. The sun is drawn upside down in the frames, so that after the roll it smiles the right way up. It is always daytime, even in a maze.

With reduced motion turned on, the banner holds still on the walk up the first corridor, crab ahead, sign in view, map to match.
