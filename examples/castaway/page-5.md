<h1 align="center">Castaway</h1>

<p align="center">

  <sub>Working title, in development, nothing published yet. An unofficial remake inspired by the 1992 screensaver <i>Johnny Castaway</i>.</sub>

**Castaway** is a stationary-frame lo-fi video for YouTube, in the spirit of the ten-hour lofi streams: a young woman alone on a tiny island with one tall palm, a raft and a lot of time. She mostly idles, nodding to the music on her headphones, and every so often something happens. A message in a bottle washes straight back. A drone delivers a parcel, and the parcel is another pair of headphones. A coconut falls on a hermit crab, and the coconut walks off with the crab inside. Sunny, hand-painted coastal anime, 16:9 at 1080p and 30 fps, always daytime.

More than 90 activities live in [activities.toml](activities.toml). Most come round on four timers, from everyday routines every 2 to 5 minutes to super-rare callbacks every 3 to 6 hours, and every one of them waits for the next bar of the music, so each gag lands on the beat. Every sound is synthesized from code by [tools/make_audio.py](tools/make_audio.py): no samples, no loops, no recordings.

```sh
python tools/serve.py
```

<summary><b>TYPE README.MD</b>: why the letters fall, and who the crab is</summary>
<br>

The banner is an ordinary DOS screen: 80 columns by 25 rows of 8 by 16 cells, light grey on black, a prompt, a page typed out with `type`. Then, as in the falling-letters screen pranks of late-1980s DOS (now kept in museum collections and shown as graphic design), the text stops holding on. Nothing on this page runs, spreads or touches a file. It is a drawing that animates, and the only thing that misbehaves is this project's own readme.

- **The o goes first.** The second o of *coconut* lets go early, lands on a hermit crab on the downbeat of bar two, and the crab leaves wearing it, a cell per 32nd note. It is the gag from the schedule, with the coconut played by a letter.
- **Then everything else.** Every grey letter lets go on a 16th note, slowly at first and then all at once, and falls a row per 32nd note. A loose letter rests on whatever is under it, including text that has not let go yet, and when it lands on a slope it slides up to two cells sideways to find a way down. The generator runs that falling-sand rule on the grid before anything is drawn and gives every letter its own short list of steps.
- **The hourglass.** The page is set narrowing line by line, like the top half of an hourglass. The letters run through the neck and pile up into the bottom half, which is where the island comes from.
- **The island.** The sea comes in over the heap a row at a time, the top of it turns to sand (the letters stay, written in it), and the sky fills in two cells at a time in random order. A palm grows out of the highest letter, she walks in over the water with an iced coffee, and the crab comes back up the beach.
- **The rest.** A palette fade to black in eight steps, then the screen is printed again, a line per 16th note, exactly as it was. Fourteen bars at 80 BPM, then round again. The title never moves.

With reduced motion switched on, the banner holds the intact screen, before anything lets go.

**How.** One SVG, built by a plain Node script with no dependencies: no fonts, no filters, no script. <summary><b>SCOPE NOTES</b>: what the traces are, and what the sound really is</summary>
<br>

**The traces.** Every key on the pad is two tones at once, a low one for its row and a high one for its column, and the scope draws each of them and their sum, 8 milliseconds across the screen. Nothing is faked: the generator adds the two real sine waves and draws the result, which is why each key's sum has its own lumpy pattern. While the line rings, the scope shows 440 and 480 Hz, the North American ringback pair. On hold it shows F and A (349 and 440 Hz), the bottom of an F major chord, drawn from the note names and not from the music file.

**The sound.** All of it comes from [tools/make_audio.py](tools/make_audio.py), more than 150 files and counting, with no samples anywhere.

It keeps the rules of the machines it imitates. The screen is 640 by 400, exactly the 80 by 25 grid of 8 by 16 character cells: the status line sits on the top row and the prompt on the bottom one. The picture uses exactly 16 colours, each a 12-bit value of the kind a 4096-colour palette allows (`#000 #fff #148 #36b #8cf #199 #6dc #fe9 #c96 #f76 #fca #742 #2a4 #052 #fec #879`), and every other shade is faked with ordered 4 by 4 dither patterns of two of them: the sky, the sea, the clouds, the sand, the title. The title, the palm, the raft, her and every visitor get a hard one-pixel black outline. The menu, the status box and the messages sit on a separate text layer that may only use the eight pure digital colours (the greyed-out TALK and the shadow under the katakana belong to the picture), from an 8 by 16 half-width font and a handful of 16 by 16 kana and kanji, all drawn pixel by pixel in the generator (yes, the zero has a slash).

The banner loops every 60 seconds, the length of the theme: 20 bars of 3 seconds at 80 BPM. <summary><b>How many hours?</b> About this screen, with credits and greetz</summary>

<br>

Switch on a Japanese home computer of the early 1980s with no disk in the drive and you did not get an error. You got BASIC, straight out of ROM. The 16-bit machines first counted their memory up to a figure followed by OK, and then BASIC opened by asking how many file buffers to reserve, from 0 to 15: a question so many people met by accident that enthusiasts still keep pages explaining it. Most of them, one suspects, pressed Enter and hoped. A row of function-key words in reverse-video boxes sat along the bottom of the screen, and anything a program drew went on a separate graphics plane underneath the text. Another family of machines showed a centred sign-on for about three seconds and then a flat blue screen.

This screen keeps all of that and changes what it means:

 FREE .............. 36000 seconds, about two thirds of them spent idling
 TITLE ............. the screen's own font, blown up a dot at a time and
                     made bold the cheap way: every dot drawn twice

- **Not copied:** no real maker, product name, version string, copyright line, font or screen was reproduced. The question, the banner, the key labels and the picture are new, and all of them are about Castaway.

Both screens come out of one small Node script with no dependencies, so they can be rebuilt and redrawn. Castaway is unofficial and is not affiliated with the makers or owners of *Johnny Castaway*. Island BASIC, its machine and both clubs in the greetz are made up.

<summary><b>ABOUT THIS BANNER</b>: how the light synth is wired</summary>

<br>

The look is a mid-1980s 8-bit light synthesizer, the joystick toy people played along to records: a black screen, a cursor, and a trail of pattern seeds that grow, change colour and fade, mirrored into a mandala on a coarse block grid, with a one-line status readout and a graduated bar at the bottom.

- **The grid.** 80 by 26 cells. The cursor runs a closed path at constant speed and loops every 15 seconds, which is five bars of the theme.
- **The pulse.** Seeds drop in time with 80 BPM. One preset drops a steady stream, six a beat, and paints rainbow wakes; the others drop two a beat, or a burst of three, and leave rows of jewels.
- **The seeds.** Each grows outward in seven levels, one per step, each level in the next colour of a seven-colour sequence, then hollows out and fades. Each seed's colours start one step on from the last beat's, so the trail reads as a travelling rainbow.
- **The symmetry.** Quad: the whole trail is mirrored through both axes.
- **The readout.** CASTAWAY in an 8x8 font drawn for this banner, the current seed shape with a tiny swatch of it, and one variable per bar with a graduated bar that wipes in to its value.
- **Kind to eyes.** A cell changes only when a ring or the cursor passes over it. Checked over every step of the loop, no cell of the trail flashes more than twice in any second, the overall brightness drifts by at most about 2% a step, and nothing blinks. With reduced motion switched on, the banner holds on its first frame.

No program's seed shapes, font or palette order were copied: the five seeds and the font are new. Two control names, pulse speed and buffer length, are kept because they say what the readout is; the values are all Castaway's. Rebuild the art with `node src/95-light-synth_opus_5.5.mjs`.

<details>
<summary><b>HOW THE FIRE IS FAKED</b>: an SVG has no frame buffer</summary>

A real feedback visualiser keeps last frame's picture and warps it. An SVG cannot remember anything from one frame to the next, so this is an imitation of feedback, not feedback, and it is honest about it.

**The echo stack.** The line is drawn 21 times. Copy *k* is the line as it was *k* × 0.08 seconds ago (its animations simply run late), moved by exactly the warp that the real thing would have applied to it since: the generator composes the flow maps for that stretch of time, numerically, and bakes the result into three animated transforms per copy. When the flow switches on a bar line, the kink travels outward through the copies, the way it does on the real screen. Older copies are dimmer and wider.

**The fire.** All the copies go through one SVG filter: fractal noise pushes them around (the push breathes in and out every 6 seconds), a blur softens them, a second, finer noise adds the speckle (the real thing carried each pixel's rounding error into the next one), and lookup tables map brightness to colour. The tables are animated, so the palette drifts. Last, a small sample is kept from every 4 × 4 block and spread over the block, so the fire is a 320 × 100 picture, as chunky as those screens were.

**The line.** The waveform is a row of half-wave lobes between fixed zero crossings, each one stretched up or down by an envelope computed from the score, so the line never breaks. The island, the palm, the fin and the bottle are drawn into the same line and burn like everything else, just at a lower heat, or the white line would vanish into its own white-hot wake. They also get a faint dark edge, for the same reason.

**The lettering** is a 5 × 7 pixel font drawn for this header, one path per glyph, never system text. **Reduced motion** shows a still: the line just after the downbeat of bar 13, its echoes where the swell has put them, palette CORAL. **Weight**: the filter repaints the whole banner every frame, so a phone or an old laptop may run it at a modest frame rate. It still looks like fire, just a calmer one.

**The idea.** The island has no plumbing. The schedule, it turns out, does. <summary><b>HOW THE BANNER WALKS</b>: a raycaster, 82 frames and one roll</summary>
<br>

The maze screensavers of the mid-1990s drew their corridors with real perspective, which an SVG cannot do: its transforms are flat. So the banner's generator, a dependency-free Node script, carries a tiny raycaster of its own. It renders the walk at 160 by 100 pixels with nearest-neighbour textures it draws itself: brick walls, floorboards, speckled ceiling tiles, four framed pictures and two life rings. The crab and the sun are flat sprites, the rock is a little flat-shaded polyhedron, and everything is depth-tested against the walls, so the crab really does disappear round corners. The sun is see-through by palette: each of its pixels is the colour behind it, mixed with sun.

One frame every sixth of a second, three frames to a step or a turn: stepped movement, which happens to be the video's own motion default. The 82 frames that differ go into one tall PNG, a film strip, and a CSS keyframe list slides it along one frame at a time. The strip is stored out of order, each frame next to the one it most resembles, because a corridor one cell further on looks almost exactly like the corridor before, and the compressor only remembers the last 32 KB, about two frames. That alone takes the strip from about 140 KB to under 90.

The rock does not turn the frames over. The frames are always rendered the right way up, and touching the rock rolls the whole view 180 degrees, zoomed in mid-roll so the corners never show. That is also why the walker turns left into the dead end: the screensaver swapped to the left hand after a rock, and so does this. The sun is drawn upside down in the frames, so that after the roll it smiles the right way up. It is always daytime, even in a maze.

With reduced motion turned on, the banner holds still on the walk up the first corridor, crab ahead, sign in view, map to match.

A stationary-frame video for YouTube, in the spirit of the ten-hour lofi streams: a young woman alone on a tiny island with one tall palm, a raft and a lot of time. She mostly idles, nodding to the music on her headphones. Every so often, something happens. A message in a bottle washes straight back. A sea turtle drops by. A coconut falls on a hermit crab, and the crab walks off wearing it. She walks out over the water and comes back with an iced coffee, because she could leave any time. Then she goes back to idling, which is most of the video, and that is the point.

**More than 90 activities** live in [`activities.toml`](activities.toml), on four timers: every 2 to 5 minutes for the everyday things, then 12 to 25 minutes, 30 to 60 minutes, and every 3 to 6 hours for the super rare ones. Every activity waits for the next bar of the music (one bar is 3 seconds), so the gags land on the beat. So do the turtles.

**Every sound is synthesized from code** in [`tools/make_audio.py`](tools/make_audio.py): more than 150 files, and not one sample, loop or recording among them, so no third-party licence applies.

```sh
python tools/serve.py
# then open http://127.0.0.1:8765/
```

<summary><b>Something happens</b>: the four timers, and what a typical ten hours holds</summary>

```text
 TIMER         COMES ROUND EVERY     IN A TYPICAL 10-HOUR RUN
 ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 regular       2 to 5 minutes        about 155
 occasional    12 to 25 minutes      about 30
 rare          30 to 60 minutes      about 13
 super rare    3 to 6 hours          about 2 (never more than 3)
 chained       when another one      follow-ups, such as the tide
               says so               after a sandcastle
 ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 typical = the median of 200 simulated runs. she is busy about a third
 of the time and idling the rest. default run: 10:00:00, seed 1992.

 ON THE BAR ..... every start snaps to the next bar of the theme, so
                  gags land on the beat (one bar = 3 s at 80 BPM)
 LANES .......... things can overlap: a ship can sail past while she
                  is busy with a coconut, which is the classic joke
 SCENE LIFE ..... 4 always-on effects and 22 timed events: shore waves
                  and drifting cloud shadows are built; birds, planes,
                  whales, dolphins, sailboats, a gecko and a rain
                  shower are planned
```

`python tools/schedule.py` validates [`activities.toml`](activities.toml) and simulates a ten-hour run.

<summary><b>Running it</b>: the renderer, the scheduler and an older reel</summary>

```text
 python tools/serve.py               the renderer: a web page with live
                                     preview, at http://127.0.0.1:8765/
 python tools/schedule.py            validate the schedule, simulate a
                                     ten-hour run
 python tools/render_demo.py --dev   a dev reel of every activity with a
                                     heads-up display (the older Python
                                     reference renderer)
```

Export happens in the browser and is frame-exact (WebCodecs H.264, 68 to 78 frames a second at 1080p30 in Chrome); the server mixes the sound and joins the two into an MP4. Hard cuts and stepped movement are the motion defaults, which is also how the turtles above get about. The page itself is [`web/index.html`](web/index.html); the working notes are in [`MUSING.md`](MUSING.md). Status: in development, nothing published yet.
